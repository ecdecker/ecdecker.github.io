As countries approach trachoma elimination, the usual sign used in prevalence
surveys—clinical grading of trachomatous inflammation—becomes harder to
interpret. In low-prevalence settings, grader disagreement or a few
misclassified cases can affect whether a district appears to cross a treatment
threshold.

Emily Decker and colleagues examined the cost of adding two biomarkers to
clinical grading: ocular swabs tested for current *Chlamydia trachomatis* (Ct)
infection, and dried blood spots (DBS) tested for antibodies that indicate
exposure and help characterize transmission.[^paper]

## Comparing real survey costs

The study used expenditure records from enhanced surveys conducted in 2022 in
four districts in Tanzania and four in Mozambique. It separated the costs of
grading, swab collection and testing, and DBS collection and testing, then
estimated an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio: the additional cost for each
additional instance of a trachoma indicator identified relative to grading
alone.

Adding both biomarkers increased the cost per cluster from $459.75 to $2,337.39
in Tanzania and from $1,381.46 to $2,147.12 in Mozambique. The percentage jump
was much larger in Tanzania, but the implementations were not identical:
Tanzania surveyed 10 clusters per evaluation unit, Mozambique surveyed 24, and
Mozambican samples were processed in the United States rather than locally.

The main cost drivers differed too. Supplies and equipment dominated in
Tanzania; per diems were largest in Mozambique. That makes the country result a
lesson about delivery systems as much as laboratory prices.

## One biomarker usually did more with the budget

At the study's point estimates, ocular swabs were the more cost-effective
addition in Tanzania: $475.25 per additional indicator identified, compared
with $572.58 for DBS. In Mozambique, DBS was more cost-effective: $323.05,
compared with $583.62 for swabs. Adding both cost $1,048.29 per additional
indicator in Tanzania and $949.34 in Mozambique, without identifying more
indicators than the strongest individual method.

The Tanzania simulations overlap substantially, with the combined method
shifted to higher cost and little added effect. Mozambique shows more
uncertainty, especially for DBS, but the combined method again occupies the
high-cost end without a corresponding gain in information.

A scenario in which Mozambique processed samples locally narrowed the gap. It
reduced the estimated cost of swabs by 15.2%, and the swab and DBS ratios became
nearly equal. That result is a useful reminder: “which test is cheapest” is not
an intrinsic property of the test. It depends on laboratory capacity, shipping,
training, staffing, and survey scale.

## Different tests answer different questions

The ratios should not be read as if swabs and DBS were substitutes in every
scientific sense. PCR testing of an ocular swab looks for current infection.
Serology from a blood spot reflects antibodies and can help estimate past or
ongoing transmission. A program may value both even when the combined option
has the least favorable ratio, because confirmation across measures can resolve
a different decision problem.

There are also no World Health Organization thresholds yet for using PCR or
serology to make trachoma program decisions. The study measures the cost of
additional information, not downstream health gains, and the new survey methods
had start-up and training costs that could fall with routine use. Results will
also change with test sensitivity, local prevalence, geography, and the way a
laboratory pools and retests samples.

The practical conclusion is therefore conditional. Enhanced testing finds
information that grading misses, but budgets and epidemiologic questions should
determine which biomarker to add. In these two implementations, choosing one
biomarker produced a better cost-to-information balance than automatically
collecting both.
