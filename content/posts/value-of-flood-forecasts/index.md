A levee is easy to see in a benefit-cost analysis: estimate the damage with and
without the structure, then compare that benefit with its cost. Forecast
information is different. It creates value only when someone receives it,
changes a decision, and improves the eventual outcome.

George Van Houtven, Emily Decker, and colleagues use Saint Paul, Minnesota, to
show how a value-of-information framework can make that chain explicit.[^paper]
Their case study follows National Weather Service hydrologic forecasts for the
Mississippi River and the city emergency managers who act on them.

## From river level to a city decision

The analysis focuses on two protective actions: closing temporary levees and
relocating impounded vehicles. For each, the researchers construct payoff
matrices that combine the cost of acting with the flood loss that could occur
at different river levels.

Historical flood-exceedance probabilities describe what emergency managers
might expect without a new forecast. Forecast information changes those
probabilities before a decision. Comparing the expected cost-plus-loss under
the two conditions gives the value of the information—not simply the damage a
flood might cause, but the mistakes a better forecast helps the city avoid.

## Most of the value came from avoiding unnecessary action

In the preferred specification, a perfect forecast produced roughly $260,000
in expected annual benefits to the city. The abstract says most of that value
came from avoided protective-action costs: not closing temporary levees or
moving vehicles when the eventual river level did not require it.

That finding broadens the usual account of early warning. A forecast can create
value by prompting timely protection, but it can also prevent expensive
overprotection. Both kinds of error belong in the same decision model.

The authors then tested forecast errors observed over the previous 30 years.
Across that range, estimated value declined by 12% or less. A forecast therefore
did not have to be perfect to retain most of its modeled benefit.

## The counterfactual carries the result

Value-of-information estimates always compare a decision with information to a
decision without it. The difficult question is what Saint Paul would actually
do without the forecast: always protect, never protect, or use some other cue.
The authors identify this counterfactual as a key source of sensitivity, along
with limits in the available cost and decision data.

That caveat is not a side issue. If the assumed no-forecast behavior changes,
the number and cost of “mistakes” change too. The case study is most useful as
a transparent method for organizing forecasts, decisions, outcomes, and values.
Its $260,000 estimate is specific to the selected Saint Paul actions and
assumptions, not a universal price for a flood forecast.
