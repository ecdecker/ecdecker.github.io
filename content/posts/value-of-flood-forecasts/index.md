A levee has an obvious place in a benefit-cost analysis: estimate flood damage
with and without the structure, then compare the avoided damage with the cost.
A forecast is harder to value. It matters only if someone receives it, changes
a decision, and gets a better result.

George Van Houtven, Emily Decker, and their coauthors use Saint Paul, Minnesota,
to show how a value-of-information framework can trace that chain.[^paper] The
case study follows National Weather Service hydrologic forecasts for the
Mississippi River and the city's emergency managers.

## From river level to a city decision

The analysis focuses on two protective actions: closing temporary levees and
relocating impounded vehicles. For each action, the researchers build payoff
matrices that combine the cost of acting with the flood loss possible at
different river levels.

Historical flood-exceedance probabilities describe what emergency managers
might expect without a new forecast. A forecast changes those probabilities
before the decision. The researchers compare expected cost and flood loss with
and without that information. The difference captures the mistakes a better
forecast could help the city avoid.

## Most of the value came from avoiding unnecessary action

In the preferred specification, a perfect forecast produced roughly $260,000 in
expected annual benefits for the city. Most of that value came from avoiding
unnecessary protective action: leaving temporary levees open or keeping
vehicles in place when the eventual river level did not require intervention.

That finding broadens the usual account of early warning. A forecast can create
value by prompting timely protection, but it can also prevent expensive
overprotection. Both kinds of error belong in the same decision model.

The authors also tested forecast errors observed over the previous 30 years.
Across that range, estimated value fell by no more than 12%. The modeled
benefit therefore remained substantial even when forecasts were imperfect.

## The counterfactual carries the result

The estimate depends on what Saint Paul would have done without the forecast:
always protected, never protected, or followed some other signal. The authors
identify that counterfactual as a major source of sensitivity, along with gaps
in the available cost and decision data. Change the assumed no-forecast
behavior, and the number and cost of the mistakes change too.

The case study is most useful as a clear way to organize forecasts, decisions,
outcomes, and values. Its $260,000 estimate belongs to these Saint Paul
actions and assumptions; it is not a universal price for flood forecasts.
